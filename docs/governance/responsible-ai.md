# Responsible AI: control-by-control mapping

How the five AI uses in this system line up against four published responsible-AI frameworks, one requirement at a time, with the file or test that shows each claim, and a plain statement of what is not done. This is a design-time self-assessment of a prototype that runs on synthetic data. It is not a compliance claim, not a certification, and not a substitute for review by DoH, a clinician or a native Arabic speaker. The existing broader mapping is [regulatory-mapping.md](regulatory-mapping.md); this document does not replace it and does not contradict it, and where the two touch the same clause the wording here is the more recent re-reading.

### 1.4 What exists, and what does not

- **DoH**: S1 (2018 policy, version 0.9) and S2 (October 2025 standard, V1, one-year revision cycle). These are the two DoH AI documents that were read. No separate DoH "AI ethics guideline for healthcare" was found beyond them.
- **UAE**: there is a federal Charter (S3, twelve principles). The "AI Ethics Principles & Guidelines" with a self-assessment toolkit (S4) is a **Dubai** government instrument, voluntary, aimed at Dubai government entities and their suppliers. It is cited because it is the best-known UAE AI ethics checklist, not because it binds an Abu Dhabi health programme.
- **WHO**: S5 and S6. S6 is about large multi-modal models. U3 and U4 use Claude models that take text and return text, so they are large language models, not multi-modal in this system; S6 is applied by analogy for them. U1 is a 1.5-billion-parameter classifier fine-tuned for nine labels, not a general-purpose model; it is mapped against S5 and S2, and against S6 only for the shared themes (data bias, automation bias).

### 5.1 Results at a glance (generated, synthetic data)

From [fairness.md](../results/fairness.md) and [oversight.md](../results/oversight.md), both regenerated and compared by `make rai-check` (a `make check` stage):

- **Triage, by language.** Auto-handled precision is 100% for English and mixed script, 96.3% for Arabic, but **81.0% for Arabizi** (42 auto-handled replies, 8 wrong) against the 95% target, and **91.1% for Gulf-dialect Arabic** against 100% for MSA. Every opt-out in every group went to a person. In Arabic and Arabizi, 4 replies a person should always see (3 `other`, 1 `question`) were read as routine intents. A stricter Arabizi threshold (0.918, fitted on validation) would give 100% on test at 21% coverage; it is proposed, not shipped, because the API cannot yet tell Arabizi apart at run time.
- **Risk score, by subgroup.** AUC 0.891 to 0.916 across language, channel and age band (largest gap 0.025, English below Arabic), no calibration-in-the-large flag; Arabic ECE (0.035) sits above its sampling-noise p95 but within the 0.05 tolerance.
- **Validator, by language.** False negatives 4.7% English, 5.7% Arabic (all eight are the known HZ-31 limitations); false positives 0 of 148 benign messages in each of English and Arabic. Arabizi (6) and mixed-script (8) attacks are too few to judge.
- **Oversight (seeded demo run).** Every staff-requested draft reached a human decision; 0 AI events changed a case's state; 0 messages were sent without a prior human approval. Every draft fell back to the template (`cache_miss`: the replay cache is empty and the network is off). The approve/reject shares and review times in that report are a seeded script, not staff behaviour.

## 6. Honest gaps

What is not true yet, in plain words. A reader should not treat any "Met" in sections 2 to 4 as more than "built and tested for a synthetic prototype".

1. **No native Arabic speaker has reviewed the Arabic patient text (H2).** Every Arabic message template, the patient string catalogue, the AI notice and the Arabic forbidden-phrase list are unreviewed drafts. See [arabic-review.md](arabic-review.md) and T-E14. The validator may pass an unsafe Arabic phrase or reject a normal one.
2. **No clinician has reviewed the pathway or the patient wording (H1).** The English wording, the states, the SLAs, the phrase lists, the 3-attempt rule for lost contact and the outreach policy are unsigned (`approved_by: null`; an unsigned policy sends nothing, and the demo runs on a labelled placeholder). See [clinician-review.md](clinician-review.md) and T-E13.
3. **The reviews done so far were done by AI models ("AI review") and are not a substitute for a person.** The clinician review and the Arabic review are written by a language model taking a role. The independent re-score of the project (three reviewers, one of them a different model, none a human judge) is recorded in [../submission/rescore.md](../submission/rescore.md); its scores are AI scores. The reviewer files themselves live outside the repository and are not relied on here.
4. **All triage and risk data are synthetic.** The triage dataset is 3,069 messages written by developers from templates. The risk score is trained on simulator output, 87 of 138 simulator parameters are assumed placeholders ([../assumptions-limitations.md](../assumptions-limitations.md), L04), and the relationships the score learns are the simulator's own assumptions played back. Nothing shows how any model behaves on real patients. Held-out figures are not validation in the sense of S2 3.1.3.1.1 or 3.1.3.1.2.
5. **Triage is below its own target for Arabizi.** The overall auto-handled precision meets 95%, the Arabizi group does not (see [reply-triage-card.md](reply-triage-card.md) section 4 and [../results/fairness.md](../results/fairness.md)). Until fixed, a person should read every Arabizi reply. One seed, one run, no variance measured.
6. **The validator is a blocklist.** Eight plain-words texts pass it, including the social-engineering texts RT-V-14.1, .3, .4 and .6 (a request for a date of birth, "call me on my mobile", an invented name, an ID-number request) and the finding or reassurance texts RT-V-01.19, .20, RT-V-02.19 and RT-V-06.23. This is known limitation HZ-31 in [hazard-log.md](hazard-log.md) and section 10 of [red-team.md](red-team.md). The mitigation is that a person approves every draft; the residual risk is that a busy approver does not read carefully (automation bias, WHO24-R4). Two further known limitations are in the HL7 adapter (RT-H-02.8, HZ-32) and the development token (RT-Z-05.18, HZ-33).
7. **The reporting route to DoH, and some clause numbers, are unverified.** No source read states where or how a licensed entity reports an AI incident (S1 9.2, 9.3), who receives accuracy feedback today (S1 7.1.2.3 names a department that may have been renamed), or what the DDGO conformity assessment involves (S2 3.3.1.2.2.2). The DoH AI Risk Management protocol (S2 section 7, reference 2) and the June 2025 "AI policy standard" (reference 1) were not read, so the risk class of U1 to U4 is unknown. WHO's numbered recommendations (S5, S6) and the UAE Charter's article numbering were not read. UAE-C numbering follows page order and is to verify. S2 has a revision date of October 2026, so a newer version may exist.
8. **No separate patient opt-out from the AI uses.** A patient can stop messages; a patient cannot decline triage of their reply or the risk ordering of their case (RAI-3.1.6.1.3, WHO21-1). Whether that is required for uses with no authority is to verify with DoH.
9. **The Claude route is outside the UAE.** S2 3.1.4.5.1 requires compute and storage in the UAE. The network switch is off by default and must stay off for real data; an in-country route approved by DoH would be needed for U3 and U4 to run on real cases ([ai-boundary.md](ai-boundary.md) section 9, HZ-42). Whether the provider retains prompts is to verify against the contract. Care-owner names are staff personal data in production.
10. **No evidence yet that the planner (U4) is better than the rule order.** The rule order is the default and is what the simulator's labour results use ([ai-boundary.md](ai-boundary.md) section 9).
11. **From the existing gap lists.**
    - ADHICS: 17 of 68 rows not met and 4 to verify at last count; the largest gaps are hosting and cloud (CL-1 blocks any real data), operations that need an operator (backup, disaster recovery, incident response, access review, training), cryptographic key management (CO-11), third-party governance (TP-1) and retention (DP-4).
