### The metrics routes (ADR-049)

Method (`tools/load/metrics_bench.py`, each phase in a fresh process so its peak RSS is its own): the replay the routes used to do (`legacy_closure` + `legacy_effort`), then the table refresh in calls of a 3 s budget until it has caught up, then the two SQL aggregates against the current table, then a refresh after 200 to 1,000 cases got an event.

| Database | Old route: closure / effort (replay of every case) | Refresh to catch up | New aggregate: closure / effort | Refresh when current | Incremental |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100,000-case scratch database (cluster Postgres) | 20.43 s / 12.89 s; RSS 924.9 / 949.9 MiB | 42 calls, 131 s total, 100,000 replays (762 cases/s), process RSS 65.9 MiB | 0.15 s / 0.017 s | 0.004 s | 200 cases touched: 200 replayed in 0.28 s |
| 1,000,000-case `closure` database (the cluster's) | not run (the replay holds every event in memory: it needed 924.9 MiB at 100,000 cases, against a 768 MiB pod limit) | 865 calls, 2,790 s total, 1,382,156 replays (495 cases/s), process RSS 61.4 MiB | 1.517 s / 0.068 s | 0.006 s | 200 cases touched: 200 replayed in 0.48 s |

Catching up at 1,000,000 cases is a one-time backfill: 865 calls of the 3 s budget (call seconds median 3.23, p95 3.43, max 3.82), 1,382,156 replays (more than the 1,000,300 cases, because the sweep's catch-up burst kept changing cases while the backfill ran), peak process RSS 61.4 MiB. The table is 316 MiB and 4,075,179 dwell rows take 695 MiB (before the two dwell indexes of migration 019). The first run's closure aggregate took 10.88 s because the median per transition sorted every dwell row; migration 019 and an index walk brought it to 1.517 s.

**Same numbers:** at 100,000 cases the old and the new answer of `closure` are equal: True; of `effort`: True (exact comparison of the whole bodies). The tests compare them exactly on seeded populations (`api/tests/test_metrics_index_pg.py`).

**What is not bounded:** the aggregate is still a scan of the table inside Postgres, so its time grows with the number of cases; a call after a large change refreshes for at most its budget and answers with the header `X-Metrics-Index-Lag`; nothing keeps the table warm between calls (a dashboard that polls does). Replay stays per case, paged and budgeted, as documented in ADR-049.

### What is still not met after M7-8

- **API pod memory is not flat.** Over 4.00 h the three pods grew by 43.85 MiB/h, 45.63 MiB/h, 45.94 MiB/h (anon, linear fits), ending at 354.2, 379.7, 348.4 MiB against a 768 MiB limit; AIC prefers the linear fit, and the slope in the last hour was 52.4, 76.6, 68.7 MiB/h. At that rate a pod would reach its limit in roughly a day; this is a slow growth, not a plateau, and its cause is not found. py-spy, memray and jemalloc are not in the image and nothing was installed; the maps of the processes (above) put the growth in anonymous memory mappings, not in glibc's `[heap]` or its 64 MiB arenas. An attempt with Python's built-in `tracemalloc` (`MEMTRACE=1`, `app.memtrace`) OOM-killed the 768 MiB pods twice (once with 12 frames per trace, once with 1 when the snapshot was taken), so it needs a larger limit; no allocation site was identified.
- The automation scan started 99.6% of its nominal starts (its work took 3.56 s on average and 14.9 s at most, gap max 20.3 s): a tick that overran was skipped, as designed. Every task met 95% of its nominal starts.
- 1 of the 48 five-minute windows of the soak had a p99 over 500 ms (worst 1836.23 ms at 2026-10-30T16:46:53.179784+00:00; overall p99 107.4 ms).
- Overload is refused, not served: at 300/s, 400/s the p99 of the requests the pods answered is over 500 ms and a share is refused with 503 (table above). Shedding turns timeouts and resets into an answer a client can act on; it adds no capacity. The web client does not yet show a message for 503 (HZ-45).
- **The metrics routes are bounded in memory and replay, not in time.** The aggregate is a scan of a table inside Postgres, so it grows with the number of cases (seconds at 1,000,000); the first catch-up of an unindexed table takes tens of minutes of budgeted calls (the benchmark's figures above); nothing keeps the table warm between calls; the agency still avoids the route above 20,000 cases (ADR-046, unchanged).
- The fail-over drill (`failover.json`) and the offline sweep and watchdog cells (`sweep.json`) were not repeated for M7-8. The scheduler's fencing and heartbeat behaviour is covered by tests (ADR-047) but a real kill, freeze and resume of the task-based scheduler on the cluster was not run.
- The target of cutting human labour by 40% is not shown to be met by anything in this report; it is a simulator result with its own caveats (docs/results).
- Everything here is one laptop-class host, a local kind cluster, one Postgres without HA or TLS, and no network latency between nodes and database. No cloud environment was measured.

### Memory growth: root cause (finding 5)

1. **Not a Python leak.** The old scan (full replay of 20,000 cases) run 7 times in one process under `tracemalloc` (`tools/load/memory_repro_tracemalloc.py`): the memory still allocated after each scan is 0.3 MiB and does not grow from scan to scan, while each scan allocates a peak of 7.8 MiB. Diffing a late snapshot against an early one finds no allocation site that grows (the largest differences are bytes). Nothing is retained by the scan.

2. **Resident memory, in the API image (Linux, glibc, scans on pool threads as FastAPI runs them), `tools/load/memory_repro_rss.py`:**

| Population | Scan | Scans | RSS after the first (MiB) | RSS after the last (MiB) | Peak RSS (MiB) | Allocator: free inside arenas at the end (MiB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000,000 cases | before: every open case id fetched into one list first | 4 | 86 | 137 | 278 | 66 |
| 20,000 cases | before: every open case id fetched into one list first | 24 | 69 | 70 | 71 | 10 |
| 1,000,000 cases | after: the incremental scan and the paged iterator | 14 | 62 | 62 | 62 | 5 |
| 20,000 cases | after: the incremental scan and the paged iterator | 24 | 62 | 62 | 62 | 5 |

Reading: the memory is not leaked; it is the transient of the old scan. `iter_open_case_models` built the list of every open case id, and the query result behind it, before replaying the first case (`ids = [r[0] for r in conn.execute(...).fetchall()]`); that is O(N) bytes allocated per scan on whichever API pod serves the request, a few hundred MiB at 1,000,000 cases against a 768 MiB limit. The allocator does not hand arenas fragmented by such a transient back to the operating system, so the pod's resident memory stays above its working set and each further scan adds to it (the table shows what this host measured). The fix removes the transient: ids are fetched 500 at a time by primary-key keyset (`iter_open_case_models`) and the scan itself reads an index, so the working set no longer depends on the number of cases. 

3. **What is NOT explained: the growth that remains.** With the scan fixed, the three API pods in the 2-hour soak still show a positive anon slope and it is the same on all three, so it is not the scan path, the agency (it runs on the leader pod only) or the watchdog. The request path was run 60 rounds of the soak's mix under `tracemalloc` and retains nothing that grows (kilobytes), so it is not a Python-level leak either. The series of the soak is a warm-up curve that bends over (see the last-40-minute slope in the Soak table), not a straight line; a 30-minute run on freshly started pods with `MALLOC_ARENA_MAX=2` (`memory_repro/arena_ab_soak30min.json`) climbed the same way, from about 70 to about 180-220 MiB in 30 minutes, so limiting glibc arenas did not remove it and was not adopted. This report therefore claims the scan-path part is fixed and the pods are not growing without bound inside the limit of 768 MiB over the 2 hours measured (maximum anon observed in the soak: see the Soak table), and does NOT claim memory is flat. A longer soak is needed to tell a plateau from a slow leak.

## What is still not met

- **The 1,000,000-case daily burst inside ONE 10 s loop.** It is bounded and drains (longest loop 6.38 s, 9 loops, 2.4 minutes at a 10 s interval), but the unbounded sweep of the same burst still takes 52.2 s with one worker on this host. Events of the last cases of such a burst are written minutes after they fell due.
- The API rate ladder: the highest passing rate with the watchdog on is 250/s; the lowest failing rate is 300/s.
- **The sweep cadence in the soak: 77.6% of the nominal sweeps completed** (period mean 12.88 s, not 10 s). A pass of the scheduler loop is the outbox drain, the sweep (0.426 s mean), the automation scan (2.974 s mean) and the evidence scan in series, and then the 10 s sleep, so the period is the interval plus about 2.9 s of work; the sweep itself is not the long part (the agency layer and the automation scan share the pod). The sweeps are bounded and steady, they are just not started every 10 s.
- **API pod memory is not flat.** The scan-path transient is gone (root cause above), but all three API pods still grew by 40 to 49.36 MiB/h (anon) over the 2.08 h soak (11.7 to 47.5 MiB/h in the last 40 minutes). No pod came near its 768 MiB limit or restarted, but the cause of the remainder is not found.
- 1 of the 25 five-minute windows of the soak had a p99 over 500 ms (worst 510.73 ms); the overall p99 was 71.68 ms.
- At 300 requests/s (the first failing stage) the API timed out and reset connections; that is overload, and it was not tuned or retried.
- **Other unbounded reads** that are not on a path measured here: `GET /metrics/closure` and `GET /metrics/effort` (and the dashboards behind them) replay every case in one call (`load_cases`); the agency's own totals avoid them above 20,000 cases, the routes do not.
- The reconciliation pass is slow by design: a full cycle at 1,000,000 cases is about a thousand scans (about 2.8 hours at a 10 s interval), so a case hidden by a wrong index value (HZ-44) is found within one cycle, not at once.
- Everything here is one laptop-class host, a local kind cluster, one Postgres without HA or TLS, and no network latency between nodes and database. No cloud environment was measured.

### Chart bugs found and fixed on apply

Applying the production chart to a real cluster exposed four defects, fixed in the chart (commits `f529e6e` and `b93af1e`):

1. **Scheduler liveness probe.** The probe required `/tmp/scheduler.json`, which only the leader writes, so a follower failed the liveness probe. A missing file now means follower and passes; a stale file (older than two minutes) still fails. The probe also gets `timeoutSeconds: 10` because Python start-up under the 250m CPU limit exceeds the 1 s default.
2. **NetworkPolicy, database CIDR.** The egress rule to the database must match the database's pod or private-endpoint address, not a Service ClusterIP (a policy is evaluated after the Service address is translated); the FHIR and IdP egress rules are now rendered only when a CIDR is set.
3. **NetworkPolicy, ingress CIDR.** There was no way to allow traffic from a node or host range, so a NodePort was unreachable from outside the cluster; the chart gained `networkPolicy.ingressCidrs`.
4. **Service `nodePort`.** The Service template could not set `nodePort`; the chart gained `api.service.nodePort` (only with `type: NodePort`).

## The watchdog scan at 100k, 500k and 1M open cases (`sweep.json`, `watchdog_cells`)

Method: the template database of each size (the sweep's seeded population, on the in-cluster Postgres) is cloned and `app.watchdog.run_incremental_scan` runs in a loop at the seed's simulated time, with the production settings (time budget 5 s per scan, a reconciliation slice of 1,000 open cases per scan, idle threshold 7 days). At that time about 39% of the seeded cases are already past the idle threshold, so the first scans are a **catch-up**: they flag every one of those cases (one `watchdog_flagged` event each), a time-budgeted batch per scan, until the index has no case left past its deadline. Then **steady-state** scans run with nothing due (only the reconciliation slice does work), and three index-only scans (no reconciliation slice). Finally the old **full replay** (`run_idle_scan`, what `POST /watchdog/scan` was) runs once on the same database: it must flag nothing more, which proves the incremental scan missed no case at that size, and its duration is the cost the scan used to have. The scan runs on the macOS host and reaches Postgres through the kind port mapping, as the sweep cells do.

## Failover drill (`failover.json`)

Tool `tools/k8s/failover_drill.py`. 5 force-delete runs, 3 graceful deletes, 3 hard kills, 3 zombie-leader (SIGSTOP) runs, each over a 240 s window with the kill (or pause) at 60 s. Per run the drill seeds 3,000 open cases whose reminder and escalation triggers fall due uniformly over the window, so the sweep always has work, and a client issues about 1 new-case flows per second (create, accept ownership, contact, acknowledge, read) through the NodePort, retrying a failed request with the same id. Lease TTL 30 s, scheduler interval 10 s, fencing true, 3 API and 2 scheduler pods.

How each mode kills: **force** = `kubectl delete pod --grace-period=0 --force` on the scheduler leader and the API pod holding the background lease (no lease release); **graceful** = ordinary delete (the processes release their leases on SIGTERM); **hard** = SIGKILL of both containers through the node's runtime, then the pods are force-deleted; **sigstop** (the zombie leader, M7-7) = the scheduler leader's process is frozen with SIGSTOP while it is mid-loop, held past the lease TTL until a new leader has taken over and swept, then resumed with SIGCONT: it wakes up in the middle of its old loop, still holding the old fencing token. Nothing is deleted. The full method text is in `failover.json`.

### Failover drill (`failover.json`)

Tool `tools/k8s/failover_drill.py`. 5 force-delete runs, 3 graceful deletes, 3 hard kills, each over a 180 s window with the kill (or pause) at 60 s. Per run the drill seeds 1,500 open cases whose reminder and escalation triggers fall due uniformly over the window, so the sweep always has work, and a client issues about 2 new-case flows per second (create, accept ownership, contact, acknowledge, read) through the NodePort, retrying a failed request with the same id. Lease TTL 30 s, scheduler interval 10 s, fencing true, 3 API and 2 scheduler pods.

How each mode kills: **force** = `kubectl delete pod --grace-period=0 --force` on the scheduler leader and the API pod holding the background lease (no lease release); **graceful** = ordinary delete (the processes release their leases on SIGTERM); **hard** = SIGKILL of both containers through the node's runtime, then the pods are force-deleted; **sigstop** (the zombie leader, M7-7) = the scheduler leader's process is frozen with SIGSTOP while it is mid-loop, held past the lease TTL until a new leader has taken over and swept, then resumed with SIGCONT: it wakes up in the middle of its old loop, still holding the old fencing token. Nothing is deleted. The full method text is in `failover.json`.

### Findings that need `api/` changes (open items, owned by the API team)

These were found by the runs above. Nothing in `api/` was changed in this work; each item states the measured evidence, not a fix.

#### 1. The watchdog scan replays every open case (O(N))

- A full replay of every case takes 224.0 and 230.4 s at 1,000,000 cases in `sweep.json` (`full_scan_seconds`; per size and worker count: 100,000 cases/1 worker(s): 9.9 s, 100,000 cases/4 worker(s): 10.8 s, 500,000 cases/1 worker(s): 74.2 s, 500,000 cases/4 worker(s): 94.1 s, 1,000,000 cases/1 worker(s): 230.4 s, 1,000,000 cases/4 worker(s): 224.0 s). The watchdog scan (`POST /watchdog/scan`) replays every open case too; `values-kind.yaml` records that it has no due-index, so a scan is O(open cases) and the cluster's own measurement of it at about 1.04M cases was 224-230 s (a comment in that file, consistent with the full-replay figures above).
- In the soak the watchdog client gave up after about 120 s in 8 of 8 scans ("timed out"); the API kept working on each, and the pod that held it rose by 181 to 255 MiB at peak and stayed more than 50 MiB above its earlier level in 3 to 7 of the 60 s samples.
- With the watchdog at its chart-default 10 s interval, scans pile up: no tested rate passes (at 50/s p99 is 2579.52 ms with the watchdog on and 124.51 ms with it off; the lowest rate tested with it on, 20/s, already fails), and requests fail from 40/s.
- Because of it the cluster runs the watchdog every 900 s instead of 10 s, which is a deviation from production (see Local cluster). At the interval of 10 s the production default is only safe while a scan is much shorter than the interval; the capacity plan below computes where that stops.

#### 2. The agency pass reads the whole case table and OOMs a pod

- `values-kind.yaml` records that the agency layer's pass reads the whole case table every 6th sweep and OOM-kills an API pod at this population, which is why the cluster runs with it off; the further observation that the pass fails at its 400-order cap comes from the run's notes. None of this is captured in the four JSON files: the soak, the latency stages and the sweep cells all ran with `agencyEverySweeps` = `0` (off). It is an observation of the run, not a number in this report.

#### 3. `GET /cases?state=` is unbounded

- One request for the 873,479 cases in `patient_acknowledged` ended in `RemoteProtocolError` after 17.4 s and an API pod was `OOMKilled` (memory limit 768 MiB); the other two pods did not restart. The endpoint answers the whole state in one response, so its cost grows with the number of cases in it; it needs pagination or a SQL-side filter (an `api/` change).
- Even bounded (the soak's worklist state held 300 cases), the worklist is the slowest endpoint: p50 154.51 ms and p99 1055.87 ms in the soak, against p99 86.04 ms for `GET /cases/{id}`.

#### 4. Duplicate and unused indexes on `events`

- `events_case_id_seq_idx` duplicates `events_case_id_seq_key` (same columns, 294 MiB each at 1,000,000 cases). Experiment on the throwaway `closure_load` database: dropping it cut the 1,000,000-case sweep from 64.18 s to 52.77 s with 1 worker (the tuned repeats ranged 60.05 to 79.43 s).
- `events_genesis_patient_ref_idx` was never scanned during the sweep (see Index findings); other code paths were not measured.

#### 5. API pod memory grows during the soak

- Anon memory of the three API pods grew at 30.2 to 130.6 MiB/h over 2.08 h (per pod in the Soak table); the worst pod, `closure-api-7b75d9d58c-v2n6v`, went from 97.5 to 302.4 MiB with a peak of 484.9 MiB against a 768 MiB limit. Extrapolating its fitted slope linearly (an extrapolation, not a measurement), it would reach the limit about 3.6 h after the end of the run. The growth is linked to the scan path: the watchdog scans and the pods they land on are the visible excursions; the slope's cause was not isolated by a separate run without the watchdog.

#### 6. The `heartbeats` table accumulates a row per scheduler pod forever

- Each follower scheduler posts a heartbeat under `scheduler-follower-<pod>`; no row is removed when the pod goes away. Rows older than 120 s whose pod is not in the cluster's pod list: first sample of the soak 2 of 3 follower rows, last sample 2 of 3 (`scheduler-follower-closure-scheduler-866cb7dd5c-5wtkl` age 8375.9 s, `scheduler-follower-closure-scheduler-866cb7dd5c-c7gr2` age 9038.8 s). Every rollout or reschedule adds one.

#### 7. The daily catch-up burst at 1,000,000 open cases does not fit in the 10 s loop on this host

- Best sweep at 1,000,000 cases: 33.64 s with 4 workers (`baseline`; with 1 worker 60.05 s to 87.30 s). The steady-state loop does fit: 0.09 to 0.22 s for the sweeps that follow the daily one, and an idle pass takes 0.10 to 1.13 s. This is a statement about the burst that follows the day's first sweep, not about every sweep.
